## What I heard

I attended the Covington Board of Commissioners caucus on Tuesday, November 10, 2026, at
6:00 p.m. in the Commission Chambers at City Hall, and I stayed for the whole thing. This
was a caucus, not a legislative meeting, so there was no public comment and no votes — the
board talked through items and moved them toward the legislative meeting on November 17,
where the ordinances get their first reading. I did not speak. I checked what I heard
against the City's published agenda and backup packet and local news coverage.

Roughly, the meeting ran:

| Approximate time | Subject |
| --- | --- |
| 00:00–01:00 | Call to order, roll call, housekeeping — two Housing Authority board appointments added |
| 01:00–07:30 | Legal Department — the mayor-council transition ordinances |
| 07:30–10:50 | People and Technology — hires and the parental leave policy |
| 10:50–11:20 | Public Works — road salt purchase |
| 11:20–13:30 | Police Department — policy updates and retiree rehire contracts |
| 13:30–14:50 | Community Development — playground contract and a youth sports licensing agreement |
| 14:50–16:50 | Economic Development — federal riverfront grant and the Convention Center land sale |
| 16:50–29:00 | Budget amendments |
| 29:00–29:50 | City Manager's report — Covington Business Council presentation request |
| 29:50–31:45 | Commissioner discussion and adjournment |

The big story of the night was the transition: Covington is rewriting its laws for a
mayor-council government that takes effect January 1, 2027, and that rewrite touches
everything from who the mayor can supervise to how often the board meets.

## What the meeting covered

**The government transition.** City Solicitor Frank Schultz walked the board through five
items from the Legal Department, all aimed at the January 1, 2027 switch from a city
manager form of government to a mayor-council form:

- An order authorizing an IRB refinancing for Duveneck Square Apartments — the City's
  $17.5 million taxable industrial building refunding bond from 2020, being refinanced
  with a new lender. Schultz said refinancings like this have happened before, that it
  would not change the City's financial arrangements, and that the payments and the term
  would stay the same. The order also makes clear the City has no responsibility for
  paying the loan or the bonds.
- A comprehensive revision of the City's code of ordinances to carry out the transition.
  Schultz said the Legal Department and the committee on the form of government
  transition have been working on it for nearly a year and a half, going through all of
  the ordinances — not only to line them up with mayor-council authority, but also to
  make substantive changes for efficiency or policy reasons. This moves to first reading
  at the legislative session.
- Amendments to Chapter 30 of the code, covering the authority and responsibilities of
  the mayor and the City Council, restrictions on communications between council members
  and city employees (to keep the executive and legislative sides separate), and other
  administrative procedures — plus updates to references and to mayoral compensation, as
  recommended by the transition committee. Commissioner Toebbe thanked Schultz for the
  chapter-by-chapter breakdown he had shared and for answering his questions quickly.
  This also moves to first reading next week.
- An ordinance setting the dates for legislative, organizational, and budget meetings —
  and repealing the current schedule. The change, as Schultz described it: the board goes
  from four meetings a month — two legislative, two caucus — to two, both legislative,
  on the second and fourth Tuesdays of each month, except for December (and possibly
  around the Fourth of July, depending on the year). The number of legislative meetings
  stays at two a month; the caucus sessions, where no public comment is taken, are
  eliminated. This moves forward too.
- A text amendment to the Neighborhood Development Code — the City's zoning code —
  changing references from city manager to city administrator or mayor, to be submitted
  for consideration by the Kenton County Planning Commission.

Local news coverage after the meeting confirms the shape of these changes: the mayor
would gain the right to sign checks, direct supervision of all city departments,
officers, and employees, and cost-of-living adjustments to the mayor's salary, while
council members would be barred from contacting city workers directly without the
mayor's authorization. Caucus meetings would be eliminated entirely — every meeting
would be legislative, meaning votes could be cast at all of them. The ordinances get
their first reading on November 17 and a final vote on December 1, according to that
coverage.

**Hires and parental leave.** Cindy Lewis, Director of People and Technology, brought five
hires and a new leave policy. Jacob Williams joins the Finance Department as Comptroller,
starting November 30. Four police officers start November 22: Noah Weber,
Elijah Freeman, and Chase Dillingham, hired as Patrol Officer–Grade 1 and
headed to the state training academy on November 29, and Chris Gallichio, a 30-year law enforcement veteran
who retired from the Newport Police Department, hired as Patrol Officer–Grade VI on a
one-year term under KRS 95.022. A commissioner asked whether the staffing figure covered
all four hires, and Lewis confirmed it did — with all four, the department stands at 112
of 114 authorized officers.

The leave policy is the one that will matter to the most people. As Lewis described it,
it creates the City's pregnancy and parental leave benefits and explains how they work
alongside FMLA, the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act, the ADA, and Kentucky's pregnant
workers law. Eligible employees get 225 hours of paid parental leave — the equivalent of
six weeks at full base pay — running concurrently with FMLA, with benefits continuing
during the leave. It covers parents who are adopting or taking in foster children, not
only birth parents, and each eligible parent gets the full benefit independently. Mayor
Washington said he was glad the policy covers adoptive and foster families. The policy
is for non-union city workers, applies to all parents, and was placed on the consent
agenda for next week's meeting.

**Road salt.** Public Works Director Bill Matteoli asked the board to approve a road salt
purchase from Compass Minerals through the Kenton County Fiscal Court's cooperative
purchasing agreement — up to 4,000 tons a year at $111.43 a ton for fiscal year 2027. It
advanced without objection.

**Police policies and retiree contracts.** Police Chief Justin Wietholter brought updates
to Police Department policies, presented for the board's annual approval. He said most
of the changes came from state legislative changes or from the department's August 2025
restructuring. He also brought four annual retiree rehire contracts under KRS 95.022 —
Wes Cook, Robert Fain, David Finan Jr., and Robert Rose, each continuing as a patrol
officer, grade six, on a one-year term. All of it advanced without objection.

**Playground and youth sports.** Walt Mace brought a $79,919.94 contract with DWA
Recreation, Inc. (doing business as GameTime) to replace the playground at Lance Corporal
Justin Sims Memorial Park in Peaselburg, funded through federal Community Development
Block Grant money. The new structure is a GameTime PrimeTime unit with a spinner and a
swing, replacing equipment that no longer meets the community's needs. The board also
advanced a licensing agreement with the Bengal Tigers Youth Football and Cheerleading,
Inc. for 47th and Decoursey Field — the Tigers have long been the field's primary users
and have made improvements there themselves. The agreement runs 20 years, can be ended
by the City on 60 days' notice, and puts the organization in charge of the field,
including maintenance, with improvements allowed with the City's approval and the chance
to pursue upgrades and grants. Both items advanced without objection.

**Riverfront grant and the Convention Center land sale.** Economic Development Director
John Sadosky brought two big Central Riverfront items:

- An agreement with the Federal Highway Administration to accept a $16,282,700 RAISE
  grant for the Central Riverfront project — the grant was applied for in fiscal year
  2024, and the agreement sets the project scope, funding, schedule, and reporting
  requirements for the public parking garage, streetscape, land bridge, and related work.
  The project is "Reconnecting Covington: Central Riverfront Hub."
- A purchase and sale agreement with the Northern Kentucky Convention Center Corporation
  for two City parcels at the Central Riverfront site — lot P plus an adjacent parking
  strip, for $3.2 million, to be used for an outdoor amenity space. It advanced without
  objection.

**Budget amendments.** Finance Director Joe Ewald presented general-fund amendments
totaling $872,000, going to first reading next week:

| Item | Amount |
| --- | ---: |
| Additional cleaning costs, new city hall | $75,000 |
| Fuel — Police Department | $95,000 |
| Fuel — Fire Department | $58,000 |
| Fuel — Public Works | $65,000 |
| Axon renewal (approved and signed, but not budgeted) | $300,000 |
| Solid waste (rate increase budgeted incorrectly) | $100,000 |
| Fire Department FEMA reimbursements, to Property Maintenance | ~$116,000 |
| Police Department training reimbursement (officer who left) | $63,000 |

Two of the items are money coming back to the city — about $116,000 in FEMA
reimbursements for the Fire Department and $63,000 in training costs for the Police
Department — leaving about $693,000 to be covered from the city's carryover from fiscal
year 2026. Ewald said the city had budgeted about $3.3 to $3.4 million in carryover and
come in above that, so the non-reimbursed part would be covered from it. Ewald also
described a state fire and EMS fund where the city had budgeted the revenue side but not
the expense side, so about $200,000 more was needed there — and he said the fund was
expected to net roughly $800,000 for the year.

A commissioner pushed back on the presentation itself, asking for a line-by-line
breakdown, for clarity on which amendments were required by a specific need and which
were precautionary cushions, and for exact figures rather than round numbers — so the
public could understand the changes too, not just the board. Ewald agreed to provide
more detail before the first reading. Another commissioner asked about the $75,000 for
cleaning: the City had originally looked at hiring someone in-house and decided not to,
so the money would go to contract cleaning of the new building. Ewald confirmed that,
and acknowledged missing the solid waste item, saying he did not take a $100,000 miss
lightly and could provide a rundown.

**Business Council and Housing Authority.** The City Manager's report included a request
from the Covington Business Council to present an update to the board on November 17; the
mayor said it would be scheduled at the top of the legislative meeting agenda. Two
Housing Authority board appointments added at the start of the meeting were also moved
to the legislative agenda.

**Commissioner discussion.** Commissioner Downing talked about a public space people enjoy
that is being revitalized, and asked for photos once the work is finished — noting that
as the weather gets colder, the space will be less usable until around the start of
March. Mayor Washington congratulated Commissioners Downing and Acri on their election
victories and said he looked forward to working with them on the council next year. A
Community Redevelopment Agency meeting followed at the close of the board meeting.

## What stood out to me

The transition is the story of this caucus. In one night, the board walked through the
laws that will rewire how Covington is governed — who the mayor supervises, who council
members can talk to, how the mayor is paid, and how often the board even meets. The board
still holds two legislative meetings a month, the same as now — what goes away is the
caucus, the meeting where no public comment is taken. From January, every meeting on the
calendar is a legislative meeting: the public can speak at all of them, and votes can be
cast at all of them. That makes showing up matter more, not less.

The budget exchange stood out for a different reason. A commissioner had to ask for the
breakdown that should have been in the presentation to begin with — which numbers were
required, which were cushions, and the exact figures. That is the right question, and it
shouldn't have to be asked.

## My view

This part is my take, not a fact about the meeting.

Two of the biggest items in the budget amendments were misses, not surprises nobody could
have seen coming. The Axon renewal — $300,000 — had been discussed, approved, and signed,
and still didn't make it into the budget. The solid waste item — $100,000 — was a rate
increase budgeted wrong. Together that's about $400,000 of the $872,000 in amendments,
nearly half of it, and both of them were the city's own numbers to get right. To his
credit, the presenter didn't dodge it — he agreed to bring a fuller breakdown before
first reading, and he owned the solid-waste miss outright, saying he didn't take a
$100,000 miss lightly.

I still think the public deserves the same breakdown the commissioner asked for. If the
city needs $872,000 in amendments, residents should be able to see, line by line, what
each dollar is for, whether it had to happen or was precautionary, and where the money is
coming from — without having to sit through a meeting to piece it together. Transparency
isn't a presentation style. It's whether a regular person can follow the money.

## Questions I'm following

**The transition ordinances** — Do they pass first reading on November 17 and final vote
on December 1? Do any of the provisions change between caucus and the vote — especially
the communications restrictions and the meeting schedule? What does the final Chapter 30
language say about the mayor's compensation?

**The budget amendments** — Do they pass first reading on November 17, and does the fuller
breakdown the presenter promised actually arrive before the vote? What does the final
budget ordinance say about the Axon renewal and the solid waste item?

**Parental leave** — Does the policy pass on the consent agenda on November 17? When does
it take effect, and does the City plan to extend it to union employees through
bargaining?

**The Convention Center sale** — Does the $3.2 million sale pass on November 17? What is
the timeline for the outdoor amenity space, and what happens to public access to that
part of the riverfront?

**The meeting schedule** — With the caucus gone, the work that used to happen there moves
into the legislative meetings. Does that change how much time the public gets to speak,
or how the meetings run? The legislative meeting on November 17 is where public comment
happens, and I plan to be there.

## What happens next

Nothing from this caucus was finally approved — a caucus is a work session, and the items
were advanced, not voted on. The ordinances go to first reading at the legislative
meeting on Tuesday, November 17, 2026, at 6:00 p.m., where public comment is on the
agenda. The transition ordinances are scheduled for a final vote on December 1, and the
mayor-council government itself takes effect January 1, 2027. I'll update this entry
after the board acts — and I'm keeping the status as "advanced from caucus" until then.
